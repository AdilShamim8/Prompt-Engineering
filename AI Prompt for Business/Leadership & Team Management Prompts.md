# Leadership & Team Management Prompts

Companion prompts to the main business guide for the human side of management: coaching, feedback, one-on-ones, delegation, and team health. These prompts draft and structure — the judgment, delivery, and accountability stay with the manager.

***

## 1. One-on-One Preparation

**Prompt:**
>Help me prepare for a one-on-one with [REPORT NAME/ROLE]. Recent context: [wins, challenges, changes affecting them]. Last conversation's follow-ups: [list].
>
>1. Which follow-ups from last time should I check on first — and what does it signal if each has stalled?
>2. Draft 5 open questions for this session, ordered from lightest to most substantive, that go beyond status updates.
>3. Flag one topic I should NOT raise this session and why (based on what's already on their plate).
>4. Draft a 3-line closing that confirms any commitments I make.

- **Purpose:** Turning one-on-ones from status meetings into the retention-critical conversations they should be.
- **Why it works:** Sequencing questions from light to substantive mirrors how trust opens up in a real conversation.
- **Use Case:** Weekly or biweekly one-on-ones, remote team management.
- **Expert Variation:**
>Add a quarterly arc: suggest themes for the next 6 sessions that build toward [DEVELOPMENT GOAL], so individual meetings accumulate into growth.

**Limitations & verification:** The model knows your report only through what you type. Its read of their state is a mirror of your observations — useful for structuring, blind to what you have not noticed.

## 2. Difficult Feedback Drafting

**Prompt:**
>Help me draft feedback for [REPORT] about [BEHAVIOR/SITUATION]. What happened: [specific facts]. Impact: [on team/work/customers]. What I want instead: [specific behavior].
>
>Draft using the SBI model (Situation-Behavior-Impact), then:
>1. One version, 5 sentences max, no euphemisms and no pile-on adjectives
>2. Anticipate their 2 most likely defensive responses, and for each, a reply that acknowledges without conceding the point
>3. One question to open the conversation that invites their perspective before my framing

- **Purpose:** Clear, fair, minimal feedback that addresses behavior without character assassination.
- **Why it works:** SBI structure strips interpretation out of the message, leaving facts that are hard to argue with.
- **Use Case:** Performance conversations, behavior corrections, quality issues.
- **Expert Variation:**
>Draft the same feedback for two different likely emotional responses (defensiveness vs. withdrawal) and adjust pacing, not content, for each.

**Limitations & verification:** Never deliver AI-drafted feedback verbatim — rehearse it aloud and re-own the words. Check your facts with anyone else involved before the conversation. For anything approaching formal performance management, involve HR before, not after.

## 3. Delegation Brief

**Prompt:**
>Draft a delegation brief for [TASK] going to [PERSON/ROLE with their experience level].
>
>Include:
>1. The outcome in one sentence — what "done well" looks like, not how to do it
>2. The constraints that are real (budget, deadline, policy) versus preferences I am willing to flex
>3. Checkpoint plan: what we review at which points, and what I explicitly do NOT need to see
>4. The decisions they own versus the ones that come to me, with the boundary stated as a threshold (e.g., "spending under $X is yours")
>5. The failure modes I am worried about, phrased as context to inform them, not rules to constrain them

- **Purpose:** Delegating outcomes instead of tasks, without abdicating oversight.
- **Why it works:** The own-versus-escalate boundary is the piece most delegation conversations skip.
- **Use Case:** Stretch assignments, manager overload, succession development.
- **Expert Variation:**
>Add a "reverse brief": the 5 questions the person should ask me before accepting, to test whether I have actually delegated.

**Limitations & verification:** Delegation failures are usually relationship failures, not brief failures. Calibrate the trust thresholds to the actual person and history.

## 4. Team Health Retrospective Design

**Prompt:**
>Design a retrospective for a team of [SIZE] after [PERIOD/EVENT, e.g., a rough quarter, a launch, a reorg]. Known tensions: [list what you know]. Goals for the session: [list].
>
>Deliver:
>1. A 60-minute agenda with timeboxes
>2. 2 exercises suited to the tensions described (specify format: silent writing, breakout pairs, full table)
>3. Anonymity mechanism for the most sensitive topic
>4. The 3 questions I should NOT ask, given the current mood
>5. A closing ritual that converts discussion into 1-3 committed actions with owners

- **Purpose:** Retrospectives that surface real issues instead of reciting safe ones.
- **Why it works:** Designing around known tensions and mood constraints respects where the team actually is.
- **Use Case:** Sprint retros, quarterly reviews, post-incident reviews (blameless variant).

**Limitations & verification:** Facilitation beats planning once the room starts. If the team does not trust you yet, bring a neutral facilitator — a great agenda cannot repair absent trust.

## 5. Managing Up: Executive Communication

**Prompt:**
>Help me communicate with my [MANAGER/EXEC] about [TOPIC]. Their context: [what they care about, their pressures, their communication style]. My goal: [decision/information/support I need].
>
>1. Draft a 150-word pre-read structured as: situation, options, my recommendation, what I need from them
>2. Anticipate their 3 hardest questions and draft one-paragraph answers
>3. Tell me which details I am tempted to include that I should cut for this audience
>4. If my recommendation serves my team but costs another team something, flag how to handle that honestly

- **Purpose:** Getting decisions from senior stakeholders without under- or over-communicating.
- **Why it works:** The "what to cut" question attacks the most common failure: engineering-level detail at executive altitude.
- **Use Case:** Project approvals, resourcing asks, escalation of blocked issues.
- **Expert Variation:**
>Produce a 30-second verbal version for a hallway encounter, and a one-slide version for a steering committee, both carrying the identical recommendation.

**Limitations & verification:** Political read is high-risk, low-verification territory. Your knowledge of the actual people dominates; where you are guessing, say so to yourself before you act on it.

## 6. Succession & Development Planning

**Prompt:**
>Act as an organizational development advisor. For the role of [ROLE] on my team, currently held by [PERSON]:
>
>1. List the 6-8 capabilities the role genuinely requires (not the incumbent's habits)
>2. Against each, note whether [PERSON] or [CANDIDATE POOL] shows evidence — and where the evidence is thin versus absent
>3. Propose a 12-month development plan with one stretch assignment per quarter, escalating in visibility
>4. Identify the single point of failure: the capability with no ready successor and no development path — and one mitigation

- **Purpose:** Building bench strength deliberately instead of discovering gaps during resignations.
- **Why it works:** Separating role requirements from the incumbent's way of doing things is the core discipline of succession work.
- **Use Case:** Growth planning, key-person risk reduction, promotion cases.
- **Expert Variation:**
>Add a "fire-drill test": describe a 2-week absence of the role's holder — what breaks first, second, third — and map each break to the development plan.

**Limitations & verification:** Capability judgments affect people's careers — they must be grounded in observed evidence you supply, never model inference. Where the model guesses, mark it and remove it. Formal succession processes belong with HR.

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Leadership Prompts

1. **Never deliver AI-drafted people communications verbatim.** Rehearse, personalize, and own the words — your team can tell the difference, and authenticity is the message.
2. **People data is sensitive.** Performance issues, health matters, and succession plans belong in HR systems and private notes, not consumer AI chats (see repository privacy guidance).
3. **Feedback, evaluation, and promotion decisions are human decisions.** AI structures the thinking; it must never make or appear to make the call.
4. **Validate your assumptions about people.** These prompts reason from what you believe about others — confirm the load-bearing beliefs directly with them when you can.
