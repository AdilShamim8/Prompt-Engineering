# Group Work & Collaboration Prompts

Companion prompts to the main students guide for the parts of student life that are graded as group output: dividing projects fairly, running meetings that end with decisions, giving teammates feedback without drama, and documenting who did what.

***

## 1. Project Kickoff & Fair Division

**Prompt:**
```
Our group project: [DESCRIBE — deliverable, deadline, course]. Team: [N people,
including strengths and constraints you know]. Help us divide it fairly:
1. Break the project into workstreams sized realistically — with dependencies
   marked (what blocks what).
2. Propose a division by capability and interest, but with a built-in check:
   which pairing puts our riskiest dependency on our least experienced member?
   Suggest a support structure for that case.
3. The accountability scaffold: what each person delivers by when — with an
   internal deadline 3 days before the real one.
4. The contribution agreement: what we do if someone cannot deliver —
   renegotiate, redistribute, or escalate to the instructor — decided NOW.
```

- **Purpose:** Starting projects with the structures that prevent their standard failure modes.
- **Why it works:** The "least experienced member on riskiest dependency" check catches the optimism that dooms week 3.
- **Use Case:** Group projects, hackathons, student org initiatives.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the integration plan: how the pieces come together — who owns the merge,
what "done" means per piece, and the format contract everyone codes to.
```

***

## 2. The Meeting That Ends in Decisions

**Prompt:**
```
Design our [30/60]-minute project meeting. Agenda input: [STATUS, DECISIONS
NEEDED, BLOCKERS].
1. Time-boxed agenda: decisions FIRST (while everyone is fresh), status
   second, brainstorming last (or cut).
2. For each decision: the one-paragraph brief I should circulate BEFORE the
   meeting, so we decide rather than discover.
3. The parking lot rule: how we capture tangents without derailing.
4. The close: decisions restated with owners and dates — the 4-line template
   for our group notes.
```

- **Purpose:** Meetings that produce decisions instead of duration.
- **Why it works:** Decisions-first ordering reverses the standard meeting decay where the important choice gets 4 exhausted minutes at the end.
- **Use Case:** Weekly project syncs, editor meetings for group writing.

***

## 3. Merge & Consistency Pass (Group Writing)

**Prompt:**
```
We are merging sections written by [N] people into one document. Paste of all
sections: [PASTE].
1. The consistency audit: terminology mismatches, repeated content, gaps where
   each writer assumed another covered it, and citation-style drift.
2. The voice report: rate the tonal difference between sections from most to
   least jarring — and tell me which transitions need bridges.
3. The redundancy table: every claim made twice, and which instance is better
   placed.
4. Do NOT rewrite the whole document — produce the fix list and let each
   author revise their own section (integrity + ownership).
```

- **Purpose:** Making group writing read as one document without one person absorbing all the editing.
- **Why it works:** Authors revise their own sections — preserving both voice authenticity and academic integrity.
- **Use Case:** Group reports, group essays, collaborative theses.

**Limitations & verification:** For graded academic work, check the course policy on AI-assisted editing — the audit-list approach keeps the editing human while using AI as the spotter.

## 4. Peer Feedback Framer

**Prompt:**
```
I need to give feedback to my teammate about [ISSUE — missed internal
deadline, quality below our agreed bar, communication style]. What happened:
[FACTS]. Help me frame it:
1. Draft the SBI version: Situation, Behavior, Impact — in 3 sentences, no
   character judgments, no "you always".
2. The listen-first opener: the question that lets them give context I might
   be missing before I deliver the framing.
3. The collaborative close: the ask phrased as a joint problem ("what would
   help you hit the internal deadline?") rather than a demand.
4. If they respond defensively: one acknowledgment that does not concede the
   point, and the exit if the conversation is not productive.
```

- **Purpose:** Peer feedback that fixes problems instead of creating them.
- **Why it works:** SBI framing plus listen-first ordering is the minimal toolkit for honest peer conversations.
- **Use Case:** Teammate issues, student org leadership, project post-mortems.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the timing check: when should this feedback NOT be given right now —
what conditions (public setting, deadline crunch, bad day signals) say wait?
```

## 5. Presentation Day Logistics

**Prompt:**
```
Our group presents on [DATE]: [FORMAT — duration, panels, demo]. Team: [WHO,
with roles]. Build the runbook:
1. The run-of-show: minute-by-minute, with speaker transitions marked and a
   buffer of [X]% for the inevitable overrun.
2. The rehearsal plan: [N] full run-throughs — which one is the "dress"
   with everything working end-to-end, including the demo.
3. The failure kit: what we do if [SLIDES DIE / DEMO BREAKS / MEMBER ILL] —
   the pre-decided fallback for each.
4. The Q&A strategy: who fields what topic, the "park and take offline"
   script for hostile tangents, and our 3 anticipated hardest questions
   with skeleton answers.
```

- **Purpose:** Group presentations where logistics do not eat the grade.
- **Why it works:** The failure kit converts presentation-day disasters into pre-made decisions.
- **Use Case:** Course presentations, demo days, conference-style student events.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the individual prep: each speaker's first and last sentence scripted,
their timing mark, and the signal for "wrap up now" from the moderator.
```

## 6. Contribution Documentation

**Prompt:**
```
Our project is done. Generate the contribution documentation:
1. From our records: [PASTE task assignments, actual delivery notes, meeting
   notes] — build the contribution summary per member: workstreams owned,
   deliverables shipped, support provided.
2. Flag discrepancies: assigned-but-undelivered, delivered-but-unassigned —
   neutrally, with the evidence, no verdicts.
3. The self-assessment scaffold: 4 questions each member answers privately
   first (what I did / what I depended on others for / what I would do
   differently / how I rate my contribution share).
4. The instructor-facing version: how we report the (sometimes uneven)
   distribution honestly if the course requires it.
```

- **Purpose:** The conversation that every group avoids and every group needs.
- **Purpose (honest version):** preventing both free-riding resentment and unrecognized carry-work.
- **Why it works:** Evidence-based, discrepancy-flagged documentation replaces the memory wars that make contribution conversations blow up.
- **Use Case:** End of group projects; peer evaluation forms; instructor debriefs.

**Limitations & verification:** The documentation is only as honest as the records — keep simple task logs during the project (the Kickoff prompt's accountability scaffold feeds directly into this).

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Group Work Prompts

1. **Decide together, in person, what AI touched.** Group integrity is collective: agree as a team what AI assistance is used, and keep it inside course policy — one member's shortcut becomes everyone's case file.
2. **Records beat memory.** Meeting notes with owners and dates, task logs, and the contribution agreement are what fairness runs on later.
3. **Feedback goes direct, not through the tool.** AI drafts the framing; the human delivers the conversation — never send a teammate an AI-written message about their work.
4. **The internal deadline is the real deadline.** The 3-day buffer between internal and real deadlines is where group projects are actually saved.
