# Academic Integrity & AI Prompts

Companion guide to the main students collection. AI is now part of student life — which makes the honest use of it a skill to learn, not a rule to memorize. This file gives you workable lines, honest reasoning about where they fall, and prompts that keep AI on the right side of them.

***

## 1. The Core Distinction: Learning vs. Producing

Most integrity policies — whatever their specific wording — draw a line that matches how learning actually works:

```
USING AI TO LEARN (generally acceptable):
- Explaining concepts you then re-derive yourself
- Quizzing you before you face the real quiz
- Critiquing a draft YOU wrote
- Helping you understand feedback you received
- Practicing problems with hints instead of answers

USING AI TO PRODUCE (generally not acceptable, unless explicitly permitted):
- Generating text you submit as your writing
- Solving problems you submit as your work
- Producing the analysis your assignment asked YOU to perform
- Summarizing readings so you never read them, when the reading IS the assignment
```

The test that cuts through all ambiguity: **what is being assessed, and did the AI do it?** If the assignment measures your writing, AI-written text defeats the measurement. If it measures your understanding, AI-generated explanation you cannot reproduce unaided is borrowed understanding — and exams have a way of repossessing borrowed things.

***

## 2. Why the Line Matters (Beyond Getting Caught)

The pragmatic case, since it is the one that actually persuades:

1. **Assessments are predictive.** Your degree signals what you can do. Skills you outsourced during assessment are missing when the signal is cashed in — in interviews, in jobs, in the follow-on course that assumes the prerequisite.
2. **The gap compounds.** Course 2 builds on Course 1's skills. Outsourced fundamentals make Course 2's work impossible to outsource safely, and the honest remediation cost grows each semester.
3. **Detection risk is real but secondary.** Institutions use detection tools, and they produce false positives and false negatives — meaning the honest student is occasionally at risk too. The stronger reason to be clean is that your integrity should not depend on detection rates.
4. **Citation skills transfer.** The habits that keep AI use honest (verification, attribution, disclosure) are the same habits that make research and professional work trustworthy.

***

## 3. Course-by-Course Calibration

Policies differ by instructor, course, and institution — and they are changing. The only reliable procedure:

```
For each course, find and record:
[ ] The syllabus AI policy (exact wording — "not permitted" vs "with disclosure"
    vs "permitted for X only" are different worlds)
[ ] The instructor's statements in class, which sometimes extend the syllabus
[ ] What each assignment's AI policy is — some allow outlines, not drafts;
    some allow drafting, not editing
[ ] Where to ask when unclear (and ask BEFORE submitting, not after)
```

**When unclear, the safe defaults:**
- Assume AI-generated submitted text is not permitted
- Assume disclosure is required even for permitted uses
- Ask — a one-line email to the instructor resolves what guesswork cannot

## 4. Prompts That Keep AI on the Right Side

**The Tutor-Only Prompt:**
```
Act as a tutor for my [COURSE] assignment on [TOPIC]. Strict rules:
1. Do not write any part of what I will submit.
2. Do not give me the answer to my assignment questions.
3. You MAY: explain the underlying concepts, ask me guiding questions, review
   my own attempts and point out errors, suggest what to read.
4. When I ask something that crosses the line, tell me and redirect to what a
   tutor could legitimately do.
```

**The Draft Critic (on my own writing):**
```
Here is MY draft: [PASTE — written entirely by me]. Do not rewrite it.
1. Tell me the two weakest arguments and why they are weak.
2. Where is my structure confusing? Quote the transition that fails.
3. What would a skeptical grader say about my evidence in paragraph [N]?
4. Grammar and style issues: list them by type — I will fix them myself.
```

**The Feedback Decoder:**
```
My instructor wrote this feedback on my work: [PASTE FEEDBACK]. Help me
understand it (not fix the work for me):
1. Unpack what each comment means in concrete terms.
2. What pattern connects the comments, if any?
3. Suggest how I practice this skill — exercises, not rewritten work.
```

**The Verification Habit (before I submit anything with AI involvement):**
```
Here is my final work: [PASTE]. Pre-submission integrity check:
1. Flag any claim that came from AI and that I have not verified against
   [MY COURSE MATERIALS].
2. Flag anything I could not explain if asked in person.
3. Draft the AI-use disclosure sentence matching my course policy: [POLICY].
```

***

## 5. Disclosure That Works

If your course permits AI with disclosure, write it plainly:

```
"In this assignment I used [TOOL] for [SPECIFIC TASKS — e.g., 'generating
practice questions for my own review' / 'critiquing my draft's structure'].
All submitted text and analysis is my own; AI-flagged points were verified
against [COURSE MATERIALS]."
```

Bad disclosures: "AI was used in this work" (too vague to be checked), and disclosures claiming the AI did less than it did (the worst of both worlds — the appearance of honesty as a cover).

Good disclosure names the tasks, the boundary you held, and the verification you did. It converts a policy requirement into a professional habit you will need for the rest of a research-adjacent career.

***

## 6. If You Have Already Crossed the Line

Practical guidance, without moralizing:

1. **Stop compounding.** Each outsourced assignment deepens the gap for the next one. The hole gets harder to climb out of with every week.
2. **Remediate before the prerequisite hits.** Identify the skills you skipped and rebuild them while the stakes are still coursework-sized.
3. **Use the amnesty that exists.** Many institutions treat disclosed first offenses and self-reports very differently from detected ones. If a conversation with an instructor or advisor is available, it is usually the cheapest exit.
4. **Change the conditions, not just the behavior.** The root cause of most academic dishonesty is time panic. Fix the schedule, fix the workload planning, get the support that addresses why the shortcut looked necessary.

***

## The One-Paragraph Version

Use AI to become smarter, not to seem smarter: tutoring, quizzing, critiquing, and explaining are on the safe side of the line because they leave the learning in your head where the degree claims it is. Writing, solving, and analyzing the things you submit are on the wrong side because they hollow out exactly what is being measured. When policies are unclear, ask before you submit; when uses are permitted, disclose them plainly; and never let a tool accumulate the skills your future self is betting on.
