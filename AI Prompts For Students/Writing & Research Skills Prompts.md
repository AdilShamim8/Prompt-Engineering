# Writing & Research Skills Prompts

Companion prompts to the main students guide for academic writing and course research: finding sources, building arguments, drafting, and revising — with the integrity boundary drawn firmly around AI's role (coach, not ghostwriter). These prompts pair with the Academic Integrity file in this collection.

***

## 1. Assignment Deconstruction

**Prompt:**
```
Here is my assignment prompt: [PASTE]. Before I plan anything:
1. Restate what is actually being asked — task verb by task verb ("analyze"
   and "summarize" and "evaluate" demand different things).
2. The grading surface: what will the grader see, in what order, and what
   does each part signal?
3. Constraints inventory: length, sources, format, citation style — every
   hard requirement listed.
4. The unasked questions: what a strong submission would need to decide that
   the prompt leaves open (scope, thesis angle, structure)?
5. The trap: where do students typically lose marks on THIS kind of
   assignment — based on what the prompt emphasizes?
```

- **Purpose:** Understanding the assignment before spending hours on the wrong one.
- **Why it works:** Task-verb analysis catches the #1 failure: doing a summary when analysis was asked (and vice versa).
- **Use Case:** Every written assignment, from short responses to term papers.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Draft the grading rubric the instructor probably has in mind — criteria and
weighting — so I can self-assess before submitting against the same yardstick.
```

***

## 2. Thesis Stress Test

**Prompt:**
```
My working thesis: [STATEMENT]. For my [COURSE/ASSIGNMENT]:
1. The opposition audit: what is the strongest case AGAINST my thesis? Who
   would make it and why?
2. The "so what" test: if true, what follows? If I cannot say why it matters,
   the thesis is a topic, not an argument.
3. The scope check: is this provable within [LENGTH] with [N SOURCES]? What
   narrower version is fully supportable?
4. Sharpen it: rewrite the thesis 3 ways — bolder, narrower, and more precise —
   without changing my core claim.
5. The falsification: what evidence would refute it, and am I prepared to
   handle that evidence if I find it?
```

- **Purpose:** A thesis that survives contact with a skeptical grader.
- **Why it works:** The opposition audit and falsification questions are exactly what strong graders probe.
- **Use Case:** Essays, research papers, thesis proposals.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Generate the essay skeleton the chosen thesis implies: what each body section
must establish, and what evidence each needs — my research shopping list.
```

## 3. Source Evaluation Coach

**Prompt:**
```
I am evaluating this source for my [COURSE] paper: [PASTE citation or
description — and abstract if you have it].
1. The credibility checklist applied: author credentials, publication venue,
   date versus my topic's currency needs, purpose (informing vs persuading
   vs selling).
2. Fit for MY claim: does this source actually support the specific point
   [MY POINT], or is it merely on-topic? On-topic is not support.
3. The corroboration question: what kind of source would I need alongside
   this one for the claim to be solid?
4. Flag: what in this source should I verify independently before citing it?
Note: you cannot confirm this source exists or is accurate — I verify in
[DATABASE/LIBRARY]. Evaluate only what I paste.
```

- **Purpose:** Source judgment — the skill that separates research from collecting.
- **Why it works:** The on-topic-vs-support distinction addresses the most common citation failure in student writing.
- **Use Case:** Any research assignment; pairs with the citation rules in the Academic Integrity file.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Build my source map: how these [N] sources relate — agreeing clusters,
disagreeing pairs, and the gap my paper addresses. Sketch the conversation,
not just a list.
```

**Limitations & verification:** The model evaluates only pasted text and cannot confirm sources exist — verification happens in the library database, always.

## 4. Reverse Outline (Structure Diagnosis)

**Prompt:**
```
Here is my draft: [PASTE]. Do a reverse outline:
1. For each paragraph: one line stating its actual claim (not what I intended —
   what it ACTUALLY argues).
2. The structure verdict: does the sequence of claims build my argument, or
   does it wander? Where does the logic skip a step the reader cannot bridge?
3. The orphan check: paragraphs that do no argumentative work — they inform,
   but nothing depends on them.
4. The balance check: which section is doing too much work (needs splitting)
   and which is coasting (needs deepening or cutting)?
5. Do NOT rewrite anything — diagnose, so I fix it myself.
```

- **Purpose:** Structural revision with editorial control kept by the writer.
- **Why it works:** Reverse outlining is the highest-leverage revision technique and works even better when a second pair of eyes does the extraction.
- **Use Case:** Second drafts of any substantial paper.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the reader-simulation: after the outline, predict the 3 questions a
careful reader would have at each section boundary — are they answered in
time?
```

***

## 5. The Revision Dialogue (My Prose, Coach's Questions)

**Prompt:**
```
Act as a writing coach for MY paper. Here is my draft: [PASTE].
You may NOT rewrite my sentences. Instead:
1. Ask me one question at a time about the weakest part you identify —
   questions that make me see the problem myself.
2. When I revise a passage, tell me if it now works and why (or why not).
3. Keep a running list of MY recurring issues (hedging, passive constructions,
   unsupported claims) so I learn the pattern, not just the fix.
4. End with the top 3 things to carry into my next paper.
```

- **Purpose:** Revision that improves the writer, not just the paper.
- **Why it works:** Socratic questioning keeps every sentence authored by the person being assessed — the integrity-safe way to get editing help.
- **Use Case:** Permitted AI-assisted revision (check course policy); final drafts.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the voice check: flag places where my draft sounds generically "AI-ish"
(template phrases, hollow transitions) — and ask me what I actually mean there.
```

## 6. Citation Workflow Guardrails

**Prompt:**
```
I am finalizing citations for my paper in [STYLE]. My sources: [PASTE LIST —
verified by me in the library database].
1. Format each in [STYLE] — and flag fields I have not supplied (page numbers,
   DOIs, access dates) as [NEED] rather than inventing them.
2. The in-text mapping: for each source, list where in my draft it is cited:
   [PASTE DRAFT CITATION MARKERS] — and flag any source in my list that my
   draft never cites, and any citation with no source.
3. The quote check: every direct quotation in my draft — [PASTE] — verify the
   citation includes location info per [STYLE].
4. Paraphrase audit: flag any paraphrase that keeps the source's sentence
   structure — I will re-do those in my own syntax.
```

- **Purpose:** Citations that pass both the format check and the integrity check.
- **Why it works:** The [NEED] rule prevents invented citation fields — the AI-specific citation failure.
- **Use Case:** Final polish before submission.

**Limitations & verification:** The model formats; it does not verify. Existence, accuracy, and quote fidelity were your job earlier in the workflow — the library database and the physical source remain the ground truth.

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Writing Prompts

1. **The words are yours.** Every prompt here is built to coach rather than write — if course policy is stricter, honor the policy; when unclear, ask the instructor.
2. **No citation ever comes from model memory.** Sources arrive from your database searches; the model only formats and cross-checks.
3. **Verify quote fidelity against the original text.** Misquotation is an error no citation style can repair.
4. **The revision loop is where learning happens.** Diagnose-then-fix beats rewrite-and-submit for your grade and for the skills the degree certifies.
