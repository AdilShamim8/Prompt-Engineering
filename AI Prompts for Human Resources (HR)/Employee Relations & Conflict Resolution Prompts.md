# Employee Relations & Conflict Resolution Prompts

Companion prompts to the main HR guide for the workplace-dynamics layer: mediating between people, conducting sensitive conversations, running investigations with fairness, and building the communication norms that prevent escalation. This is HR's most judgment-intensive territory — the prompts structure process; they never render findings or decisions.

***

## 1. Conflict Intake & Triage

**Prompt:**
```
Act as an employee relations advisor. A conflict has been raised: [DESCRIBE
THE PRESENTING SITUATION — who is in conflict (roles, not names), what each
party says, duration, business impact so far].
1. The triage classification: interpersonal friction / process dispute /
   performance issue presenting as conflict / values or conduct issue /
   manager-employee dynamic / potential policy violation.
2. The urgency assessment: what harm is actively occurring, and what
   escalates if this waits two weeks versus being handled this week?
3. The route: which conflicts resolve with manager coaching, which need
   facilitated conversation, which need formal process — and the
   misdiagnosis risks (treating a conduct issue as a personality clash
   is the classic error).
4. The preliminary questions I need answered before choosing a route —
   and who holds each answer.
```

- **Purpose:** Correct routing before anything else — the most consequential ER decision happens first.
- **Why it works:** The misdiagnosis warnings target the classic failures: conduct issues defanged as "personality conflicts", and normal friction over-processed into formal cases.
- **Use Case:** Any conflict intake; manager escalation requests.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the pattern check: is this the first instance or the third time this
configuration of people/roles has produced conflict? Repeat patterns
usually indicate a system cause, not a personal one.
```

**Limitations & verification:** Triage routes decisions involve legal exposure (harassment, discrimination, retaliation) — where those are plausible, formal process and legal counsel lead from the start; this triage organizes intake, never substitutes.

## 2. Facilitated Conversation Design

**Prompt:**
```
Design a facilitated conversation between two parties in conflict.
Situation: [ROLES + each party's account + what each wants]. My read of
the underlying interests (vs. positions): [YOUR ANALYSIS].
1. The pre-work: individual conversations with each party first — ground
   rules, their goals for the joint session, and hearing each side
   without committing to outcomes.
2. The session structure: opening frame (purpose: understanding and a
   working relationship, NOT adjudication), uninterrupted speaking time
   each, reflection-back of what was heard before moving to problem-
   solving.
3. The facilitation moves for the hard moments: when one party
   interrupts, when blame-spirals start, when the conversation surfaces
   something that should move to formal process instead.
4. The agreement scaffold: what a workable outcome looks like — specific
   behaviors, not friendship; revisit date; what happens if it frays.
5. The exit ramp: how to end without agreement constructively.
```

- **Purpose:** Structured mediation that repairs working relationships without pretending to adjudicate.
- **Why it works:** Reflection-before-problem-solving and behavior-based agreements are the two mechanics that keep facilitated conversations from re-enacting the conflict.
- **Use Case:** Peer conflicts; team friction; cross-functional disputes.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the follow-up design: the 2-week and 6-week check-ins — what I ask
each party separately, and the signals that the agreement is or is not
holding.
```

## 3. Sensitive Conversation Preparation

**Prompt:**
```
Prepare me for a sensitive employee relations conversation: [TOPIC — e.g.,
discussing a complaint about hygiene, addressing suspected personal
struggles, responding to a disclosure].
1. The opening: first 2 sentences verbatim — private setting, care-first
   framing, no ambush.
2. The core questions: how I invite their perspective without pressing
   for disclosures they do not owe me — the line between support and
   interrogation.
3. The response map: for the likely reactions (relief, embarrassment,
   denial, anger, tears), what I do in the first 10 seconds of each.
4. What I can offer: the concrete support options — EAP, accommodations,
   schedule flexibility, time off — stated as options, not prescriptions.
5. The boundary honesty: what stays confidential, what I must act on, and
   when I must say so — told to them upfront, not discovered later.
```

- **Purpose:** Humane, clear handling of the conversations HR people lose sleep over.
- **Why it works:** The boundary-honesty section protects the trust that sensitive conversations run on — the "why didn't you tell me" betrayal is worse than the hard topic.
- **Use Case:** Personal-issue conversations; disclosures; wellbeing concerns.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the aftermath plan: what documentation is appropriate (factual,
minimal, stored per policy), and my own debrief — these conversations
accumulate; processing them professionally is part of doing them well.
```

## 4. Workplace Investigation Structure

**Prompt:**
```
Structure a fair workplace investigation. Complaint: [DESCRIBE — nature,
who is involved (roles), when reported]. Formality level: [informal
inquiry / formal investigation].
1. The investigation plan: allegations restated as questions to answer,
   evidence sources per question (documents, systems data, witnesses),
   and the interview sequence — with the rationale for the order
   (typically: complainant, witnesses, respondent last).
2. The interview protocol: open questions, one topic at a time, no
   leading; documenting close to verbatim; and the standard advisories
   (confidentiality expectations, no retaliation warning, honesty
   obligations).
3. The credibility framework: how I will weigh conflicting accounts —
   consistency, corroboration, specificity, plausibility — applied
   uniformly to all parties.
4. The findings discipline: facts found / facts not determinable /
   policy implications — written separately from recommendations, which
   belong to the decision-maker, not the investigator.
5. The process safeguards: timeliness, communication to both parties
   about status, documentation standards throughout.
```

- **Purpose:** Investigations whose fairness survives scrutiny — by parties, courts, and hindsight.
- **Why it works:** Separating findings from recommendations is the structural protection against investigator overreach; the credibility framework makes judgment explicit and uniform.
- **Use Case:** Policy violation complaints; harassment and discrimination matters (with counsel); grievance processes.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the pre-mortem: how could this investigation be challenged later —
by the complainant, the respondent, an external reviewer? Design the
safeguard for each vulnerability now.
```

**Limitations & verification:** Investigations touching discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or legal exposure require your organization's formal process and, typically, legal counsel from the outset. This structure supports the investigator's craft within that process — it does not define when the formal process applies.

## 5. Team Climate Intervention

**Prompt:**
```
A team's climate has degraded: [SYMPTOMS — attrition, conflict frequency,
disengagement signals, feedback patterns]. Team context: [FUNCTION, SIZE,
HISTORY — reorg/growth/leadership change].
1. The diagnosis layer: what the symptom pattern suggests about
   underlying causes — leadership behavior, workload, role ambiguity,
   fairness perceptions, interdependence problems — and what data would
   distinguish them (exit data, skip-levels, engagement signals).
2. The intervention options, matched to cause: facilitated team session /
   leadership coaching / workload restructuring / role clarity reset /
   process redesign — with the honest note that some causes are
   structural and no amount of team-building fixes them.
3. The session design if facilitation is the route: ground rules that
   make honesty safe, the anonymous-input collection beforehand, and how
   leadership participates without dominating.
4. The measurement: what changes in the observable signals over the
   following quarter — defined BEFORE the intervention.
```

- **Purpose:** Climate problems addressed at their actual layer — behavior, structure, or leadership — rather than universally treated with morale events.
- **Why it works:** The "some causes are structural" honesty prevents the most common climate-intervention failure: pizza for a workload problem.
- **Use Case:** Post-reorg recovery; attrition clusters; engagement dips.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the leadership mirror: how to surface to the team leader that their
behavior is part of the pattern — the conversation that climate work
lives or dies on, and the one most often skipped.
```

## 6. Difficult Communication Drafting (ER Context)

**Prompt:**
```
Draft a difficult ER communication: [TYPE — a warning about behavior, a
decision response to a complaint, an organizational change affecting
someone personally].
Facts to convey: [THE FACTS]. Tone requirements: [respectful, direct, no
legalese, no false warmth].
1. The draft: 150-250 words — says what must be said, once, clearly;
   no euphemisms that obscure, no threats beyond what policy states.
2. The deletion audit: what I am tempted to add that should NOT be in
   writing (speculation, character commentary, promises, apologies
   that imply liability where unintended).
3. The delivery plan: medium (some messages must be spoken first,
   written second), setting, timing, and who is present.
4. The acknowledgment: what the recipient signs/receives, and the record
   this creates.
5. The legal flag: if this touches [termination, discrimination claims,
   protected activity], mark it for counsel review before sending.
```

- **Purpose:** ER communications that are humane without being legally careless.
- **Why it works:** The deletion audit is where ER writing goes wrong — most damage comes from what got added in the heat of drafting, not what got said.
- **Use Case:** Warning letters; complaint responses; change communications with individual impact.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Run the future-reader test: this may be read later by a lawyer, a judge,
or a journalist. Rewrite anything that reads differently under that
light — clarity that survives all audiences is the standard.
```

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Employee Relations Prompts

1. **Formal processes and legal counsel lead where exposure exists.** Discrimination, harassment, retaliation, and termination matters follow your organization's process and legal guidance from intake — these prompts support the craft, never the routing decisions.
2. **Anonymize relentlessly.** ER matters are the most sensitive data class in HR — roles and patterns only, in approved tools (the main guide's privacy rules apply at maximum strength here).
3. **The investigator is not the judge.** Findings, discipline, and decisions belong to the designated decision-maker through the defined process — separation of roles is the fairness mechanism.
4. **People hear the conversation, not the framework.** Whatever is prepared here gets delivered by a human being with genuine care — scripts structure; they never perform.
