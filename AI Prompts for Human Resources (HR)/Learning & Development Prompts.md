# Learning & Development Prompts

Companion prompts to the main HR guide for building people's capabilities: identifying real skill gaps, designing development plans that survive busy quarters, career-path conversations, and training that transfers to the job. All individual development data follows the main guide's privacy rules — anonymize before prompting.

***

## 1. Skill Gap Analysis (Team Level)

**Prompt:**
```
Act as an L&D strategist. Our team: [FUNCTION, SIZE, SENIORITY MIX]. Strategic
direction for the next 18 months: [WHERE THE WORK IS HEADING — new tech, new
markets, new ways of working].
1. The capability map: skills the direction REQUIRES, versus skills the team
   demonstrably has (from work evidence, not self-report) — present as a
   matrix with gap severity.
2. The build/buy/borrow decision per critical gap: develop internally, hire,
   or contract — with the realistic cost and speed of each.
3. The risk ranking: which gap, unaddressed, breaks the strategy first?
4. Sequencing: what to develop NOW versus what can wait — development
   capacity is finite and competes with delivery work.
5. The measurement trap warning: how NOT to measure this program
   (completion rates) — and what to measure instead (capability applied
   in real work).
```

- **Purpose:** Development investment aimed at strategic capability, not trendy courses.
- **Why it works:** Build/buy/borrow framing prevents the default of sending everyone to training when the honest answer for some skills is hiring.
- **Use Case:** Annual L&D planning; reorganizations; technology transitions.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the hidden-capability audit: skills team members have demonstrated
outside their formal role — often the cheapest gap to close is already
in the building, underused.
```

## 2. Individual Development Plan (Realistic Version)

**Prompt:**
```
Help me build a development plan for an employee (anonymized): [CURRENT
CAPABILITIES + EVIDENCE, CAREER INTEREST, ROLE CONTEXT, TIME AVAILABLE —
e.g., "2 hours/week"].
1. The growth goal, stated as observable capability: "able to X
   independently by [WHEN]" — not "improve at X".
2. The 70/20/10 structure: on-the-job assignments (the 70%), coaching and
   relationships (the 20%), formal learning (the 10%) — concrete per
   component, with named projects and people where possible.
3. The practice design: how they will get REPS with feedback — capability
   comes from repeated doing plus correction, never from consumption
   alone.
4. The manager's role: what support I actually commit to (specific,
   scheduled) versus what sounds nice.
5. The tripwires: what would tell us at 6 weeks that the plan is not
   working — and the pre-agreed adjustment.
```

- **Purpose:** Development plans that build capability instead of documenting intentions.
- **Why it works:** The reps-with-feedback requirement and pre-agreed tripwires convert the IDP from a filing-cabinet document into a working protocol.
- **Use Case:** Post-review development; retention conversations; promotion preparation.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the sponsorship layer: who beyond the manager advocates for this
person's visibility — assignments and opportunities travel through
sponsorship, not just skill.
```

**Limitations & verification:** The plan serves the employee's growth — its existence is their personal data. Draft with anonymized context in AI tools; the real plan lives in your HR system and, more importantly, in the working relationship.

## 3. Career Path Conversation Guide

**Prompt:**
```
Prepare a career conversation for: [ANONYMIZED CONTEXT — their interests,
their strengths evidence, realistic organizational possibilities].
1. The exploration questions: what energizes them, what they want more and
   less of, where they see themselves in 2-3 years — asked without
   pre-loading a path.
2. The honest landscape: what growth exists HERE (vertical, lateral,
   expertise-deepening, scope-widening) — and what paths the organization
   genuinely cannot offer soon.
3. The stretch mapping: their evidence of strengths mapped to roles/
   directions they may not have considered — offered as hypotheses, not
   verdicts.
4. The two-way honesty: what the employee must do to be ready for their
   preferred direction — and what the organization must do to make it
   real. Both sides named.
5. The commitment capture: 2-3 concrete next steps with owners — including
   what I (HR/manager) will do.
```

- **Purpose:** Career conversations that build trust and retention — even when the honest answer is "that path doesn't exist here."
- **Why it works:** The honest-landscape section prevents the conversation failure that costs the most trust: implying paths that will never open.
- **Use Case:** Retention-risk conversations; development cycles; stay interviews.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the regret-prevention check: if this person resigned tomorrow, what
would we wish we had offered? Sometimes the answer changes the
conversation's priorities legitimately.
```

## 4. Training Design That Transfers

**Prompt:**
```
Design training that transfers to the job. Training need: [SKILL]. Audience:
[WHO — roles, prior knowledge, dispersion]. Constraints: [FORMAT PREFERENCE,
BUDGET, TIME].
1. The performance objective: what participants will DO differently on the
   job — the observable behavior, not the content covered.
2. The practice core: how the training is built around realistic practice
   with feedback (role-plays, worked problems, real-case labs) rather
   than presentation-first design.
3. The spacing plan: single events decay; design the follow-up touches —
   practice assignments, refresher micro-sessions, peer check-ins over the
   following 60 days.
4. The manager involvement: what participants' managers do before (set
   expectations) and after (create application opportunities) — training
   without manager follow-through is expensive theater.
5. The evaluation ladder: how we measure at reaction / learning / behavior
   levels — with behavior-level (transfer) as the standard we commit to.
```

- **Purpose:** Training budgets that change workplace behavior.
- **Why it works:** Practice-first design, spaced follow-up, and manager involvement are the three factors that most reliably separate transferring training from enjoyable training.
- **Use Case:** Any internal training program; vendor program evaluation (apply the same criteria).
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the build-versus-buy test: given this design's practice core, is a
vendor actually delivering it, or content coverage with a practice label?
Generate the vendor evaluation questions that expose the difference.
```

## 5. Mentorship Program Structure

**Prompt:**
```
Design a mentorship program for [POPULATION — e.g., early-career engineers /
new managers / returners]. Organizational context: [SIZE, CULTURE, WHETHER
FIRST TIME].
1. The matching logic: what criteria drive pairs (development goal fit,
   not just function or seniority) — and the escape hatch for
   non-functioning matches without awkwardness.
2. The structure: session cadence, suggested duration of the relationship,
   and the light-touch agreement pairs complete (goals, confidentiality,
   meeting rhythm).
3. The mentor preparation: a 1-page brief — what mentoring is/is not,
   listening practices, boundary-setting, and the difference between
   advice and sponsorship.
4. The program's honest scope: what mentorship solves and what it cannot
   (it complements, never substitutes for, management and structural
   equity).
5. The health metrics: match survival, participant-reported growth
   actions taken — NOT satisfaction alone.
```

- **Purpose:** Mentorship that develops people instead of decorating an org chart.
- **Why it works:** The escape hatch and honest-scope sections address the two standard program failures: trapped bad matches and mentorship as a substitute for fixing structural problems.
- **Use Case:** New programs; fixing existing ones.

## 6. L&D Program Evaluation (Honest Version)

**Prompt:**
```
Evaluate our L&D program honestly. The program: [DESCRIBE — what, who,
cost, participation]. Evidence available: [COMPLETION, SATISFACTION, ANY
BEHAVIOR OR OUTCOME DATA].
1. The evaluation ladder, honestly climbed: reaction (did they like it),
   learning (did they know more), behavior (did they do differently),
   results (did it matter) — where does our evidence actually reach?
   Flag the gap between where it reaches and what we've been claiming.
2. The counterfactual question: what would have happened without the
   program? (Skills develop naturally; what did WE add?)
3. The cost-honesty pass: total cost including participant time — versus
   the evidence of impact at the highest rung we can actually support.
4. The verdict options: continue / continue-modified / redesign /
   sunset — with the reasoning shown for each, and no default toward
   continuation.
5. What data to start collecting now for a real evaluation next cycle.
```

- **Purpose:** L&D accountability that resists the program-preservation instinct.
- **Why it works:** The counterfactual question and the no-default-to-continuation rule counteract the two forces that keep ineffective programs alive: sunk cost and stakeholder comfort.
- **Use Case:** Annual L&D review; budget cycles; post-program assessment.

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All L&D Prompts

1. **Anonymize individuals before prompting.** Development interests and career aspirations are sensitive personal data — patterns and roles are enough for these prompts (see the main guide's privacy rules).
2. **Development is an agreement, not an assignment.** Plans built without the employee's voice are compliance documents; these prompts prepare the conversation, never replace it.
3. **Opportunity is part of the design.** Skills without assignments to apply them decay; check that real work opportunities accompany every plan.
4. **Measure behavior, not activity.** Completion and satisfaction are the beginning of evaluation, not its conclusion.
