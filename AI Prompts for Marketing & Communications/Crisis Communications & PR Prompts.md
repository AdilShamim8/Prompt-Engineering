# Crisis Communications & PR Prompts

Companion prompts to the main marketing guide for the highest-stakes communications work: crisis response, issues management, media relations, and the preparedness that makes the difference. The structural rule for everything here: AI drafts and rehearses — speed and judgment in a live crisis belong to humans with authority and full facts.

***

## 1. Crisis Response Framework (The First Two Hours)

**Prompt:**
```
Act as a crisis communications advisor. Situation: [WHAT HAPPENED — known
facts, scale, who is affected, whether it is evolving]. Our organization:
[CONTEXT]. Current knowledge state: [WHAT IS CONFIRMED vs UNCONFIRMED].
Build the first-two-hours response:
1. The holding statement: acknowledges awareness, states what we're doing,
   commits to a next update with a specific time — under 100 words,
   saying nothing we may need to retract.
2. The stakeholder triage: audiences in order of who must hear from us
   first (affected customers before press, employees before public
   where feasible) — with the channel and rough message per audience.
3. The facts discipline: the running list of what is CONFIRMED /
   UNCONFIRMED / FALSE (rumor control) — single source of truth
   format.
4. The "do not say" list: speculation on cause, blame-shifting,
   minimization, premature "fully resolved" claims.
5. The escalation triggers: what developments move this to the next
   severity tier.
```

- **Purpose:** The first hours that determine whether the story is the incident or the cover-up.
- **Why it works:** The confirmed/unconfirmed/false discipline and "do not say" list prevent the two classic crisis failures: guessing publicly and minimizing dishonestly.
- **Use Case:** Service outages, data incidents, product safety, public controversy.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the dark-site specification: the pre-built page this crisis would
activate — structure, approval path (pre-approved NOW for speed), and
the URL strategy.
```

**Limitations & verification:** Legal exposure (data breaches, safety, regulatory) means counsel is in the loop from hour zero — this framework structures communications work that runs alongside, never instead of, legal process.

## 2. Holding Statement Drafting

**Prompt:**
```
Draft the holding statement for: [CRISIS TYPE + confirmed facts only].
Constraints: [AUDIENCE, CHANNEL, LEGAL REVIEW STATUS].
1. Three versions: 50 words (social), 100 words (web/media), 150 words
   (internal memo) — same facts, calibrated depth.
2. The structure test: awareness acknowledged → action stated → next
   update time committed → care for affected expressed. Anything
   missing?
3. The tone calibration: concerned competence — not defensive, not
   theatrical. Flag any phrase that reads as corporate distance
   ("we regret any inconvenience" is the classic tell).
4. The retraction audit: every sentence checked — could new facts make
   this false? Remove or soften anything that forecasts ("isolated
   incident", "no data was affected") unless literally confirmed.
5. What we are NOT saying yet, and why — documented for the decision-
   maker, not just omitted.
```

- **Purpose:** Say enough, early, without saying anything the next day contradicts.
- **Why it works:** The retraction audit operationalizes the golden rule of crisis statements: never let the update contradict the original.
- **Use Case:** Any developing incident.

## 3. The Crisis Simulation (Tabletop)

**Prompt:**
```
Run a crisis simulation for our team. Scenario: [CHOOSE — data breach /
product recall / executive misconduct allegation / viral customer incident
— or I will ask you to surprise me within our realistic risk set].
1. Set the scene: inject the news as it would actually arrive (social
   post, journalist email, internal discovery) — with realistic
   ambiguity about facts.
2. Run it in 4 moves, each injecting new information that changes the
   picture: what does the team do at each stage? (I will describe our
   decisions; you play the world — press, customers, regulators —
   and their realistic reactions.)
3. After: the debrief — where we hesitated, what we lacked (pre-drafted
   materials? decision authority? monitoring?), and the top 3
   preparedness gaps.
4. The improvement list: what we prepare NOW so the real one goes
   better — templates, chains of approval, monitoring setup.
```

- **Purpose:** Rehearsing crisis response while the stakes are zero.
- **Why it works:** Injects with "realistic ambiguity" train the actual skill — deciding with incomplete information — which no static playbook teaches.
- **Use Case:** Team preparedness; onboarding comms leaders; annual refresh.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the hostile-inject round: the journalist who has a partially wrong
version and a deadline, the executive whose first instinct is silence,
the viral thread misrepresenting the facts — our responses, one by one.
```

## 4. Media Response Preparation

**Prompt:**
```
Prepare me for a media inquiry: [OUTLET, JOURNALIST'S KNOWN APPROACH,
DEADLINE, WHAT THEY'VE ASKED].
1. The response decision: engage / statement-only / decline — with the
   honest calculus for each path (declining does not make stories
   disappear; it removes our voice from them).
2. The message house: our 3 core messages, each with supporting facts
   and the proof points that make them credible.
3. The difficult questions: the 8 hardest questions this journalist
   could ask — including the ones whose true answers are bad — with
   bridging responses that stay honest ("Here's what I can tell you
   now... here's what we're still determining").
4. The delivery discipline: what I say on the record, on background,
   and not at all — decided before the call, not during it.
5. The pitfalls: quotes that become headlines (flag any drafted phrase
   that works out of context), the "no comment" trap, and speculating
   under friendly pressure.
```

- **Purpose:** Media engagement that serves the story honestly without becoming its casualty.
- **Why it works:** Pre-deciding the record/background/silent boundaries and rehearsing the bad-answer questions — the two disciplines that fail under deadline pressure without them.
- **Use Case:** Journalist inquiries; interview prep; proactive media outreach.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the written-versus-spoken decision: when a written statement serves
better than an interview — and how to decline the interview while
still serving the story the journalist can actually print.
```

**Limitations & verification:** Media strategy for legally sensitive matters runs through counsel first; "no comment" versus substantive response in a legal context is a legal judgment, not a communications preference.

## 5. Issues Management (The Pre-Crisis Layer)

**Prompt:**
```
Build our issues radar: the slow-burn problems that become crises if
unmanaged. Our context: [INDUSTRY, KNOWN VULNERABILITIES, CURRENT
SIGNALS — reviews, forums, support themes, social sentiment].
1. The issue inventory: 5-8 plausible issues ranked by likelihood ×
   damage — from our actual vulnerabilities, not generic ones.
2. For each: the early indicators (what we'd see first — where, who
   would notice, what tool would catch it), and the threshold where
   "monitoring" becomes "acting".
3. The pre-positioning: for the top 3, what we can do NOW — fix the
   underlying issue, prepare the materials, build the relationships —
   while it is cheap.
4. The ownership: who watches each issue — a name per row, or it is
   nobody's job and therefore nobody's.
```

- **Purpose:** Managing problems while they are still cheap and quiet.
- **Why it works:** Most "sudden" crises were visible issues for months — the radar with named owners is the difference between management and surprise.
- **Use Case:** Quarterly risk reviews; post-incident hardening.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the issue-to-crisis conversion test: for each top issue, the single
event that would ignite it (a lawsuit, a journalist's question, a viral
post) — and whether we'd survive that ignition with current
preparations.
```

## 6. Post-Crisis Review & Recovery

**Prompt:**
```
The acute phase is over. Design the post-crisis review and recovery:
[WHAT HAPPENED + HOW IT ENDED + CURRENT SENTIMENT/STATE].
1. The blameless review: timeline reconstruction, decision points, what
   information was available when — focused on system improvement,
   not fault-finding (people hide information in fault-finding
   cultures, and the next crisis gets worse).
2. The external close-out: do stakeholders get a final update, a
   what-changed summary, or silence? The calculus per audience — and
   the "here's what we fixed" message that converts crisis response
   into trust signal.
3. The trust-rebuild plan: for affected customers specifically — the
   concrete actions, not gestures, that rebuild what was damaged.
4. The institutionalization: what this crisis taught that becomes
   permanent — new monitoring, new templates, new decision rights —
   so the lesson survives the team's memory.
```

- **Purpose:** Extracting full value from the crisis: a better organization, and possibly stronger stakeholder trust, on the other side.
- **Why it works:** The blameless-review principle and "what we fixed" close-out convert crises from pure loss into partial investment — organizations that do this visibly earn durable trust.
- **Use Case:** Post-incident; annual review of past crises.

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Crisis & PR Prompts

1. **Counsel sits in the loop for anything with legal exposure.** Data incidents, safety, regulatory matters, and litigation-adjacent situations: legal review precedes publication — every time, without exception.
2. **Facts come from the source of truth.** In a live crisis, AI works only from facts you supply and mark confirmed — never from its own understanding of the situation.
3. **Rehearse with real ambiguity.** Simulations that hand the team clean facts train nothing; demand the messy versions.
4. **The spokesperson is a human with authority.** AI drafts statements, anticipates questions, and structures responses — delivery, judgment calls, and the accountability stay with people empowered to make them.
