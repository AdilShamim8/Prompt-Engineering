# Differentiated Instruction Prompts

Companion prompts to the main teachers guide for teaching one lesson to many readiness levels: scaffolding the same rigorous content for different entry points, without creating a three-tier classroom of haves and have-nots. The core principle throughout: differentiate the PATH, never the destination.

***

## 1. Tiered Task Design (Same Objective, On-Ramps)

**Prompt:**
```
My lesson objective: [OBJECTIVE]. My class: [DESCRIBE RANGE — e.g., readers
spanning 3 grade levels, newcomers to the language alongside fluent speakers].
Design tiered tasks:
1. The destination is IDENTICAL for all tiers: [RESTATE OBJECTIVE].
2. Three on-ramps that vary the SCAFFOLD, not the rigor:
   - Support tier: sentence starters, partially worked examples, chunked
     directions, key vocabulary pre-taught
   - Core tier: standard scaffolds
   - Extension tier: same task plus a "now complicate it" dimension
     (apply to a harder case, justify to a skeptic, find the exception)
3. For each tier: what the teacher prepares, what students do, and how the
   tier exits to the same assessment.
4. The equivalence check: could a parent read all three and say "same
   lesson"? If not, flag what drifted.
```

- **Purpose:** Differentiation that respects every learner's right to the same rigorous objective.
- **Why it works:** The equivalence check prevents the classic failure — support tiers quietly becoming lower-expectation tracks.
- **Use Case:** Mixed-readiness classrooms, language-diverse classes, compacting.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Design the movement plan: how a student on the support tier this week earns
the core tier next week — what evidence, what conversation.
```

## 2. Scaffold Fading Plan

**Prompt:**
```
I am scaffolding [SKILL/TASK] for [GRADE/SUBJECT]. Build me the fading plan:
1. Full scaffold: worked example with annotations — students see expert
   thinking narrated.
2. Partial scaffold: worked example with steps missing — students supply
   the reasoning at the gaps (mark which gaps).
3. Hint-first: no scaffold visible; hints available on request — design the
   3 hint levels (nudge / direction / near-answer).
4. Independent: the task alone, with a self-check list.
5. The fade schedule: how I decide when a learner moves between stages —
   evidence, not calendar.
```

- **Purpose:** Scaffolds as temporary structures with planned removal.
- **Why it works:** The evidence-based fade (worked example → completion → hints → independent) matches how skill acquisition actually progresses; scaffolds that never fade become crutches that define students.
- **Use Case:** Writing genres, problem-solving procedures, lab skills, research skills.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the re-entry rule: when a student regresses (illness, disruption, gap),
how they re-enter at the right level — without shame or starting over.
```

## 3. Flexible Grouping Strategy

**Prompt:**
```
Design my grouping strategy for [CLASS, N STUDENTS] across a [UNIT].
1. The rotation plan: groups by readiness (for targeted instruction), by
   interest (for engagement), and mixed (for peer teaching) — scheduled by
   lesson type, with the rationale for each.
2. The dignity rules: how groups form WITHOUT public labeling — no visible
   "levels", group names that carry no rank, membership that visibly rotates.
3. The roles: within-task roles that rotate (facilitator, recorder,
   questioner, skeptic) so every student practices every function.
4. The small-group instruction window: while groups work, which group meets
   with me, for what purpose, on what cycle — so targeted teaching reaches
   everyone who needs it during the unit.
```

- **Purpose:** Grouping that targets needs without stratifying the classroom socially.
- **Why it works:** The dignity rules directly attack the hidden cost of ability grouping — students always know, and labels persist.
- **Use Case:** Reading instruction, math workshop, project units.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the regrouping triggers: what evidence during the unit says a student's
group placement is wrong — in both directions.
```

## 4. Question Ladder for Whole-Class Discussion

**Prompt:**
```
My lesson topic: [TOPIC]. Objective: [OBJECTIVE]. Build my question ladder
for a discussion that includes every readiness level:
1. Rung 1 — Entry: questions every student can answer (recall, observe,
   connect to experience) — the confidence floor.
2. Rung 2 — Processing: compare, explain, apply — the core of the objective.
3. Rung 3 — Stretch: evaluate, synthesize, take a position — for students
   ready to go further.
4. The participation engineering: how I sequence (entry question to a
   partner first, then share out — so processing time is built in), and how
   I hand off between rungs so students climb rather than sort.
5. The follow-up moves: for each rung, my follow-up question that pushes
   one level deeper without abandoning the student.
```

- **Purpose:** Discussions where every student enters and no student ceilings.
- **Why it works:** The ladder keeps rigor in whole-class settings where differentiation is hardest — the entry rung protects struggling learners, the stretch rung protects advanced ones from boredom-induced disengagement.
- **Use Case:** Seminars, text discussions, math talks, current events.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the accountable-talk stems per rung: the sentence starters that let a
less-confident student operate at a higher rung than they would unaided.
```

## 5. Compacting for Advanced Learners

**Prompt:**
```
I have students who have already mastered [OBJECTIVE] — evidence: [PRE-ASSESSMENT
RESULTS or work samples]. Design the compacting plan:
1. The exemption evidence: what exactly they test out of, and how the pre-
   assessment proves it (not vibes — evidence).
2. The replacement work: deeper on the SAME objective (not just "read ahead"
   or more of the same kind of problem) — design 2 options with genuine
   intellectual stretch.
3. The anchor requirement: what they still do with the class (discussions,
   collaborative tasks) so compacting does not equal isolation.
4. The check-back schedule: how I verify the compacted path is working —
   and the re-entry path if it is not.
```

- **Purpose:** Advanced learners spared relearning what they know, without drift or isolation.
- **Why it works:** "Deeper, not just faster" and the anchor requirement prevent compacting's two standard failures: enrichment as busywork-lite and social estrangement.
- **Use Case:** High-readiness students in any mixed classroom; also adaptable for students with deep expertise in one domain (e.g., native speakers in language classes).
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Design the mentor role: how compacted students can legitimately help peers
(teaching, not answer-giving) — with the framing that keeps it good for both
sides.
```

## 6. The Differentiation Reality Audit

**Prompt:**
```
Audit my differentiation plan for sustainability and equality:
[DESCRIBE PLAN — tiers, groups, scaffolds, timelines].
1. Workload reality: what does this plan cost me per week in preparation?
   Which piece gives the most access for the least cost — and which piece is
   theater (feels differentiated, changes nothing)?
2. The equity audit: which tier/group gets the most of my face time during
   the week? Is that distribution what my data says the students need?
3. The expectations audit: for each tier, are the long-term expectations
   identical or have they quietly diverged? Where might a support-tier
   student be under-served by the design itself?
4. The 80/20 redesign: the version of this plan that survives a bad week —
   what to keep, what to pause, what to automate.
```

- **Purpose:** Differentiation that a real teacher sustains past October.
- **Why it works:** The "theater" question and 80/20 redesign acknowledge the truth every teacher knows: unsustainable differentiation collapses into nothing, which serves no one.
- **Use Case:** Unit planning, mid-course correction, coaching conversations.

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Differentiation Prompts

1. **Same destination, different roads.** Every prompt above enforces it, but the audit is yours: if any tier's endgame is a lesser objective, the differentiation has failed its purpose.
2. **Know the learners, not the categories.** AI works from what you describe; your knowledge of the actual children — their strengths, histories, and what dignifies them — overrides any generated plan.
3. **Labels are load-bearing.** Nothing that publicly marks a student as "lower" belongs in the design; rotate groups, name them neutrally, and let movement be visible.
4. **Sustainability is part of fidelity.** The differentiated plan you abandon in November helps no one — design the version you can run in your worst week, and expand from there.
