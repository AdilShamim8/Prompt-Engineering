# Classroom Discussion & Engagement Prompts

Companion prompts to the main teachers guide for the participation layer of teaching: discussions that include everyone, activities that carry learning (not just energy), transitions that do not leak minutes, and the engagement of students who have learned school means sitting quietly.

***

## 1. Discussion Design for Full Participation

**Prompt:**
```
Design a discussion on [TOPIC] for [GRADE/CLASS SIZE, 25-40 minutes].
Goals: [CONTENT OBJECTIVE + participation goal — e.g., "every student speaks
or writes-once before minute 30"].
1. The sequence: warm-up pair talk (2 min, everyone speaks) → small groups
   (4-5 students) → whole-class synthesis. Timing per stage, adding to my
   total.
2. The question ladder: entry questions anyone can answer through stretch
   questions — 6-8 total, ordered.
3. The talk structures inside small groups: roles (facilitator, skeptic,
   recorder, connector) with one-line job descriptions.
4. The equity moves: how I call on students (volunteers + prepared cards),
   wait time of 3+ seconds stated as a rule, and the "pass then return"
   convention.
5. The synthesis: what gets captured where, so the discussion produces a
   class artifact rather than evaporating.
```

- **Purpose:** Discussions designed for participation, not performance by the confident few.
- **Why it works:** The pair-then-group-then-class ramp gives every student two low-stakes speaking rounds before the high-stakes one — the structural fix for discussion inequity.
- **Use Case:** Seminars, text discussions, ethics/current-events conversations.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the silent-start variant: the first 5 minutes are written (everyone
responds in writing first) — for classes where the same five voices open
every discussion.
```

## 2. Think-Pair-Share, Engineered Properly

**Prompt:**
```
Upgrade my think-pair-share for [LESSON/QUESTION]:
1. The think phase: the prompt written AND displayed; silent solo time with
   jotting (specify minutes — it is always longer than teachers think);
   a word or sentence frame for students who blank.
2. The pair phase: the instruction that prevents turn-and-talk collapse —
   who speaks first (rock-paper-scissors, quick rule), the listen-then-
   respond requirement ("start with: I agree/I disagree because..."), and
   the 1-minute-per-person structure.
3. The share phase: how pairs report (random selection via cards; report
   your PARTNER'S idea — forces real listening).
4. The accountability artifact: 30 seconds of writing after the share —
   "what did you hear that changed or sharpened your thinking?"
5. Total time budget that sums correctly for [MY MINUTES].
```

- **Purpose:** The workhorse structure tuned so it does actual cognitive work.
- **Why it works:** The report-your-partner's-idea move and post-share writing convert social time into accountable thinking — the two upgrades TPS most often lacks.
- **Use Case:** Every subject, every week — worth engineering once.

## 3. The Cold-Call Alternative Kit

**Prompt:**
```
I want the accountability of random calling without the fear that makes it
cruel. For [CLASS PROFILE]:
1. The warm-call system: students get the question IN ADVANCE of random
   selection ("In 3 minutes I'll ask 3 of you: [question] — everyone
   prepares").
2. The no-opt-out with dignity: when a student passes or blanks, the
   sequence: come back to them after another answer, or offer the choices
   version (two options instead of open recall).
3. The participation tracking that is invisible to students: how I quietly
   ensure the rotation covers everyone across a week without a public
   chart.
4. The framing script: the one-sentence norm statement that makes random
   calling feel like inclusion ("everyone's thinking is worth hearing")
   rather than ambush.
```

- **Purpose:** Broad participation without the dread that shuts down exactly the students who most need to talk.
- **Why it works:** Warm-calling and no-opt-out-with-dignity preserve the accountability while removing the fear that makes cold-calling counterproductive for marginal participants.
- **Use Case:** Secondary classrooms, discussion-heavy courses.

## 4. Activity Design: Engagement That Isn't Noise

**Prompt:**
```
I am designing an activity for [OBJECTIVE]. My constraint: the activity must
require the objective's THINKING, not just produce classroom energy.
1. Audit my idea: [DESCRIBE ACTIVITY IDEA] — does the fun part contain the
   cognitive work, or is it packaging around a worksheet? Be honest.
2. The redesign: same engagement level, but the central move students make
   is [THE OBJECTIVE'S VERB — analyze/evaluate/justify].
3. The structure: setup minutes, work time, share-out — with the "aha"
   moment placed where the learning happens, not the end.
4. The struggle calibration: where students will get stuck productively,
   and my prepared hint levels that unstick without resolving.
5. The exit artifact: what each student produces that shows whether the
   objective landed.
```

- **Purpose:** Activities where engagement and learning are the same thing.
- **Why it works:** The "does the fun part contain the thinking" audit is the single best filter for activity design — busy and learning are different phenomena.
- **Use Case:** Review days, project launches, any lesson needing energy.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the zombie-test: if I walked in with no context, would this activity
look like learning or like a party? What one change makes it legible?
```

## 5. Transitions & Dead-Time Audit

**Prompt:**
```
Audit my lesson flow for lost minutes: [PASTE LESSON PLAN with timings].
1. Transition inventory: every point where students change activity —
   estimated real cost (including settling, materials, questions) versus
   my planned cost. Where do the hidden minutes live?
2. The transition routines that pay back: consistent signals, materials
   protocols, and the "sponge" activities for the students who finish
   entering before the others.
3. The dead-time diagnosis: moments where most students wait while a few
   are managed — distribution questions, logging in, finding pages.
   Batch-fix each.
4. The recovered-minutes plan: what those minutes become — the discussion
   time or practice time that was being silently sacrificed.
```

- **Purpose:** Recovering the 8-12 minutes per lesson that transitions quietly eat.
- **Why it works:** Transition time is invisible in plans and enormous in practice — the audit makes the leak measurable before fixing it.
- **Use Case:** Any lesson that reliably runs out of time; classroom management refresh.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Design the timing check for ME: the two timestamps I jot during delivery
that tell me whether the plan is tracking — mid-lesson self-monitoring
without a clock obsession.
```

## 6. Reaching the Disengaged (Systematically)

**Prompt:**
```
I have students who have disengaged: [DESCRIBE PATTERNS — heads down,
never speak, work avoidance — WITHOUT names or identifying details].
Help me respond systematically:
1. The diagnostic lens: for each pattern, the most common underlying causes
   (skill gaps making work humiliating, home circumstances, peer dynamics,
   learned helplessness from repeated failure, boredom from mastery) — and
   the evidence that would distinguish them.
2. The re-engagement moves matched to cause — first-month moves for each:
   early wins, competence signaling, role assignment, challenge rather
   than rescue.
3. The relational sequence: the 2-minute individual conversation script —
   curiosity, not confrontation ("I've noticed X, what's going on for you
   with Y?") — and what I do with what I learn.
4. The escalation boundary: signs that this needs [COUNSELOR/SUPPORT TEAM/
   FAMILY] rather than classroom strategy — and how I document what I
   tried.
```

- **Purpose:** Disengagement treated as information, not defiance.
- **Why it works:** The cause-matching prevents the universal (and useless) response to all disengagement: more consequences.
- **Use Case:** Any classroom, any point in the year; especially early-year pattern-setting.
- **Expert Variation:**
```
Add the invisible-engagement check: how I notice whether a quiet student is
disengaged or merely introverted-and-working — and why treating those
identically fails one of them.
```

**Limitations & verification:** This prompt works from patterns you describe, without identifying details (see the Student Privacy file — that boundary applies here too). Support teams, counselors, and families hold the information and authority for the serious cases; classroom strategy and escalation work together.

***

## Shared Verification Rules for All Engagement Prompts

1. **Engagement must carry the objective.** Energy is a delivery vehicle, not the cargo — every structure above ends with the learning artifact, not the fun.
2. **The class you have overrides the template.** Adapt norms, signals, and structures to your actual students and school culture — you know them; the prompt does not.
3. **Participation is designed, not wished for.** Structures (ramps, roles, warm-calls) move the distribution; exhortations rarely do.
4. **No identifying student details in prompts.** Patterns, not people — the privacy rules from this collection's privacy file apply to everything here.
