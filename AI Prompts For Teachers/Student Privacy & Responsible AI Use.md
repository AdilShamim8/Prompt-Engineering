# Student Privacy & Responsible AI Use

Companion guide to the main teachers collection. Teachers work with two sensitive things at once: children's data and children's trust. AI tools can genuinely reduce teacher workload — but only if used in ways that protect both. This file draws the practical lines.

***

## 1. The Data That Cannot Go In

Student information is protected by law in most jurisdictions (FERPA in the US, GDPR provisions for minors in Europe, and equivalents elsewhere) and by school policy everywhere. The baseline that satisfies all of them:

**Never paste into consumer AI tools:**
- Student names, initials combined with identifying details, ID numbers, photos, or recordings
- Grades, IEPs/504 plans, health information, behavioral records, counselor notes
- Written student work with names attached (even first names — small classes make re-identification trivial)
- Family contact information, custody situations, home circumstances
- Anything from a student record system, screenshot or otherwise

**The re-identification trap:** replacing a name with "Student A" helps, but in a class of 24, the description "Student A, who struggles with reading and just moved schools" re-identifies instantly. True anonymization means removing the details that make the person findable, not just the name.

**The safe pattern:**
```
Before: "My student Jake's essay shows he still reverses b and d, and his
mother mentioned the recent move..."
After:  "A 2nd grader's writing sample shows common letter reversals following
a mid-year school transition — help me design support activities."
The teaching question survives. The child disappears from the record.
```

## 2. What AI Is Actually Good For (The Teacher Side)

Within those boundaries, teachers can capture real workload relief:

**Safe and effective:**
- Drafting lesson structures, unit plans, and activity ideas from your objectives
- Generating practice sets, worked examples, and alternate explanations
- Rubric drafts, question audits, blueprint design (see the Assessment file)
- Formatting, leveling, and differentiating YOUR materials (see the Accessibility file)
- Rehearsing difficult conversations with hypothetical scenarios
- Summarizing teaching resources YOU paste (articles, curriculum docs)

**Requires anonymization:**
- Analyzing class-level performance patterns (strip names, use small-n caution)
- Feedback drafting (paste the work, never the identity)
- Parent communication drafts (describe the situation, not the family)

**Never:**
- Automated grading decisions presented as final
- Screening, ranking, or evaluating students with AI
- Behavior prediction or "risk scoring" of any kind
- Any use your district policy prohibits — policies vary and tighten frequently

## 3. Students Using AI: Your Classroom Policy

Students will use AI regardless of policy — the question is what your classroom's honest relationship with it looks like:

**A workable classroom AI policy covers:**
1. **Per-assignment clarity** — "no AI," "AI for brainstorming only, disclose it," or "AI permitted for X with citation" per task, stated on the assignment sheet, not assumed
2. **The learning rationale** — students accept boundaries they understand: "this essay assesses YOUR argumentation, so AI drafting defeats the measurement" works better than "because I said so"
3. **A disclosure convention** — a standard sentence students append when permitted AI is used, normalizing honesty instead of driving it underground
4. **The verification habit** — when AI use is permitted, students also show the verification they did ("I checked the three statistics cited against my textbook")
5. **Age-appropriate reality** — younger students need the privacy conversation too: what they type into tools, and why their data matters

**The strategic view:** the students who learn to use AI well — as tutor, critic, and practice partner rather than ghostwriter — leave your course with a genuine skill. Teaching that distinction beats prohibiting the tool and hoping.

## 4. AI-Generated Content: The Verification Duty

Material you did not write has failure modes you must catch before it reaches your class:

```
Before using AI-generated instructional content:
[ ] Factual claims checked against your curriculum materials — models
    confidently produce outdated or wrong facts
[ ] Reading level actually verified with the text in front of you
[ ] Names, examples, and contexts reviewed for bias and cultural fit
    (see the Accessibility file's representation audit)
[ ] Math and science answers recomputed — generated worked examples
    sometimes contain confident errors
[ ] Question sets piloted — ambiguity and wrong keys do surface
[ ] Age-appropriateness confirmed for YOUR students, not "students"
```

The pattern from every other file in this repository applies with extra weight: AI proposes, the teacher verifies, and the teacher's name on the material means the teacher checked it. Students cannot distinguish your errors from your endorsements — a wrong "fact" in your handout carries your credibility.

## 5. Tool Selection Questions

Before adopting any AI tool for classroom use, the questions that matter:

1. **Data handling**: What does the vendor do with inputs — retention, training use, third-party sharing? Is there a contractual education agreement (FERPA/DPA-aligned)? Consumer versions of tools usually fail here; education editions often pass.
2. **Age requirements**: Does the tool's terms of service even permit your students' age group? Many general AI tools require users to be 13+ or 16+ or 18+ — classroom use with younger students may violate the terms outright.
3. **District approval**: Does your school or district have an approved-tools list or review process? Using unapproved tools with student data can put you personally at risk, however good the intent.
4. **The alternative**: For this specific task, is AI actually better than the non-AI path — or just newer? The planning minutes saved mean little if the verification minutes double.

**When in doubt, ask before adopting**: your IT department, data protection officer, or administrator would always rather answer a question than unwind an incident.

## 6. A Classroom Reality Check (The Two-Minute Version)

```
MY STUDENTS' DATA:
[ ] No identifiable student data has entered a consumer AI tool — this year,
    including "just first names" and "just a quick question"

MY MATERIALS:
[ ] Everything AI-generated that I distributed was verified against course
    content before it went out — facts, answers, examples

MY POLICY:
[ ] Students know, per assignment, what AI use is permitted and how to
    disclose it
[ ] I have explained the WHY, not just the rule

MY TOOLS:
[ ] Anything students touch is age-appropriate per its terms and approved
    by my school — or I have asked
```

The teachers who run through that list quarterly are the ones who get the workload benefits of AI without ever being the cautionary tale in a district newsletter.

***

## Where the Rules Come From

This file draws its boundaries from widely shared principles — educational privacy law, minors' data protection, and professional ethics — but **your jurisdiction and district policy are the binding versions**. Regulations in this space evolve quickly, and school implementations vary. Before a borderline case becomes a decision, check the current policy or ask the person who owns it. When policy is silent, the strictest reasonable reading protects both your students and you.
